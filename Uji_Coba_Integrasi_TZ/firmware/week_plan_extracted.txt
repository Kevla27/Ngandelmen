What changed in this revision
1. No EVAL-ADE9000 board will be purchased; the team goes straight to its own hardware. 2. The one-week embedded onboarding is already complete, so week 1 starts on real
work rather than training. Together these pull the spin 1 fabrication release forward by roughly three weeks and add real margin at the back end — but they also mean no metrology
firmware can run on hardware until spin 1 arrives.

One honest note on cost
A batch of spin 1 boards will not be cheaper than a single evaluation board — PCB, assembly, stencil and components at low volume usually exceed it. The justification for going
direct is capability and schedule: the team learns the full design flow earlier, and the board matches our actual sensing design rather than ADI's demonstration one. If the decision
was made purely on cost, it is worth re-checking the arithmetic before the fab order goes out.

Quantity is the cheapest risk reduction in this plan
Two boards means the team queues for hardware and one mistake halts a lane. Eight to ten boards means every engineer develops in parallel, someone can cut a track to test a

anyway.

3. Firmware Before Hardware Exists
Weeks 1–8 have no ADE9000 hardware in the building. That time is not lost — it is spent building the parts of the metrology firmware that do not need a chip, in a form that
can be tested on a workstation. This is the same discipline the onboarding week established with the command parser: separate logic that can be tested on a PC from code
that must touch a register.
Work item
What it is
How it is verified without hardware
Register map header
Every ADE9000 register, address, bit field and reset value transcribed from
the datasheet into a header file
Second-person review against the datasheet, line by line. This is tedious and it is exactly
the kind of error that costs a week during bring-up
SPI transaction layer
Read, write, and burst-read functions with the device's framing and CRC
handling
Unit tests on the host against a mock SPI that replays known byte sequences from the
datasheet's timing examples
Device mock
A software model of the ADE9000 that answers register reads with
configurable values and can inject synthetic waveform data
Lets the whole metrology pipeline — read, scale, accumulate, report — run and be tested
on a workstation in CI
Scaling and accumulation logic
Conversion from raw codes to volts, amps, watts and kWh, including the
fixed-point arithmetic
Host tests with known inputs and hand-computed expected outputs. Overflow and rounding
behaviour tested here, not discovered on hardware
Calibration procedure draft
The sequence and the arithmetic for gain, phase and offset calibration per
channel
Dry-run against the mock with simulated errors injected, so the procedure is exercised
before it meets a real board
Bring-up script
The exact ordered list of what to read and write on first power-up, with
expected values
Reviewed by both mentors. Turns day one with a new board from improvisation into a
checklist
When spin 1 arrives in week 8, the team should be swapping the mock for the real SPI driver, not starting to write a driver. If the mock work is done properly, the first
meaningful register read happens within days of the boards landing rather than weeks.
Where to get the reference material without buying the board
ADI publishes the ADE9000 evaluation board schematic, layout guidance and user guide as free documentation. The reference schematic is the starting point for our analog front
end whether or not we own the hardware — download it in week 1 and design from it rather than from a blank sheet.

7. KiCad Discipline (carried forward, unchanged)

Version control from day one. KiCad project in Git alongside the firmware; every review and fab release is a tagged commit.

ERC and DRC in CI. kicad-cli runs on every push, exactly as the firmware tests do; a failing check blocks the merge.

Library discipline. Custom symbols and footprints for the ADE9000 (LFCSP) and STM32U575VGT6 (LQFP100) built once, verified against datasheet drawings by a second
person.

Footprint verification ritual. Every new footprint printed 1:1 and physically checked against the real component before layout. This single habit prevents the most common
and most expensive novice error — and with no eval board to fall back on, a wrong footprint now costs the whole metrology lane, not just a board.

Manufacturer rules first. Stackup, trace and space, drill sizes, test point and fiducial requirements encoded as KiCad design rules before layout begins.

Two-person review of every sheet. No schematic sheet goes to layout reviewed only by its author.

Four-layer stackup minimum, with a continuous ground reference under the analog section and under the SPI bus.

DFM round trip before the fab order, with a week budgeted on each spin.

12. What This Revision Asks You to Accept

--- PAGE 10 ---
Revision D is the tightest-coupled plan of the four. Removing the evaluation board removes the safety net underneath the metrology lane: if spin 1 has a serious fault,
firmware and hardware stall together. In exchange, the completed onboarding buys back three weeks, so the back end of the schedule now has genuine margin —
calibration and the accuracy comparison sit in weeks 22–23 rather than being squeezed against the handoff.
Two things decide whether this works. The first is the blind firmware work in section 3: done properly, spin 1 goes from boards-in-hand to real register reads in days. Done
carelessly, the team starts writing a driver in week 9 and the plan loses its margin immediately. The second is building spin 1 in quantity — it is the cheapest insurance
available against the risk this revision introduces.
